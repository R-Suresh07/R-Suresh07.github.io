## Introduction

An agent harness is everything that surrounds the model: the tools it can call, the state it can see, and how observations are presented back to it. An agent therefore does not act on the environment directly. Its actions pass through an interface that decides what capabilities are available, while observations and results are transformed before they return to the model.

This means an agent system contains two decision procedures rather than one. The model decides what to do with the information it receives. The harness decides **what information reaches the model, what gets remembered, and which actions are possible in the first place.**

The asymmetry is that these two procedures are optimized very differently. The model’s policy is learned through training, while the harness around it is still commonly designed by hand. A developer decides which tools to expose, what state deserves a durable representation, how outputs should be structured, when context should be compacted, and what conditions should block an action or submission.

Those choices rely heavily on **human taste**. Usually, we run an agent, inspect where it failed, form a hypothesis about whether the problem came from the model or its environment, modify the harness, and run everything again. The final fix can be a few lines of code. Figuring out which few lines to change can take days.

This raises the question behind this post. **How far can harness engineering take us before the remaining bottleneck is the model’s scientific policy itself?** And if harness engineering is another iterative search process, how much of that search actually requires a human?

Our testing ground is the Small Molecule Drug Design benchmark, SMDD-Bench ([Han et al., 2026](#han-et-al-2026)), a collection of challenging, multi-turn agentic tasks in small-molecule design. Coding and math give agents relatively cheap and direct feedback when something goes wrong. Generative chemistry is far messier, because evaluating a candidate can require specialized scientific models, the feedback is imperfect, and several tasks depend on reasoning over 3D molecular structure.<Sidenote number="1">Boltz-2, which we shorten to Boltz below, lets us estimate whether a molecule is likely to bind to a protein without running a physical experiment. It is much cheaper than traditional simulation methods, but its predictions are still imperfect and can vary across protein targets.</Sidenote>

A task is also more than a text prompt. The agent may receive a 3D protein structure, the location of its binding pocket, or the 3D position of part of a molecule. None of this is naturally easy for a language model to interpret. **What the model knows about the scientific problem therefore depends heavily on what the harness computes, remembers, and chooses to show it.**

We focus on three SMDD-Bench tasks that expose different kinds of harness dependence. We first engineer their harnesses by hand, then ask whether an automated optimizer can discover similar improvements.

## Our testbed: SMDD-Bench

SMDD-Bench contains 502 instances across five small-molecule design tasks. Each combines chemical and biological reasoning with specialized tools, and several also require reasoning over the 3D geometry of a protein binding pocket.

We focus on Interaction Point Discovery, Lead Optimization, and Scaffold Hopping. They sit at very different points on the current capability curve. Lead Optimization is comparatively tractable, with the best reported model solving 57.6% of tasks, while the best reported pass rates for Scaffold Hopping and Interaction Point Discovery are only 3.8% and 4.0%, respectively.<Sidenote number="2" align="paragraph">These are the best reported pass rates in the original SMDD-Bench evaluation: GPT-5.4 for Lead Optimization, GPT-5.4 / Claude Sonnet 4.6 / DeepSeek V3.2 tied for Scaffold Hopping, and Gemini 3.1 Pro for Interaction Point Discovery.</Sidenote> This gives us one task where agents already succeed reasonably often and two where performance remains close to the floor.

## Interaction Point Discovery

The goal of Interaction Point Discovery (IPD) is to identify valuable interaction hotspots inside a protein binding pocket. These hotspots mark regions that small molecules repeatedly exploit when binding to the same protein, making them useful for fragment growth, molecular optimization, and computational screening.

The task has an unusual information asymmetry. **The agent receives a single receptor structure and the location of its binding pocket, but the hidden answer is derived from interactions recurring across many different bound ligands.** The agent must predict three ligand-side interaction points, specifying both their 3D coordinates and pharmacophore type.

This means the task is not simply asking which interactions look chemically plausible in the visible receptor. It asks which of those possibilities are likely to remain useful across a diverse set of binders. The agent also has only three predictions, so each one must identify both the right interaction type and approximately the right location in 3D space.

### How well do current agents do?

Not great. Across the models evaluated in the original SMDD-Bench, only Gemini 3.1 Pro solved a single IPD task, while every other model scored 0/25. Our additional Qwen 3.5 9B baseline also scored 0/25. **However, the all-or-nothing task metric hides a more informative pattern: several models recover one or two of the three required interaction points without ever completing a task.**

<IpdBaselineFigure />

This partial recovery matters because it suggests that the models are not uniformly failing at every part of the task. To understand what prevents plausible partial predictions from becoming complete solutions, we inspected the failed trajectories directly.

### What are the agents getting wrong?

One common strategy that the models used was to inspect the receptor.pdb, identify chemically plausible interaction sites around the pocket, and treat those as likely conserved hotspots. But **plausibility is not the same as conservation**. A binding pocket can contain many chemically attractive places for a ligand to interact, but what we actually care about is which of those possibilities are repeatedly used by different molecules binding to the same protein. This might sound like a small distinction, but it changes the problem considerably. A site can look perfectly reasonable from the receptor structure and still rarely recur across real binders.

A more surprising failure was that agents sometimes predicted the wrong side of the interaction altogether. The interaction points belong to the ligand, not the receptor itself. For example, if the protein exposes a hydrogen-bond donor, the target is not the donor atom itself, but the location where a ligand acceptor would sit. The agent therefore has to infer what complementary feature should occupy the empty pocket space based on the surrounding protein chemistry. This difference turned out to matter much more than we initially expected.

Even when agents identified sensible regions of the pocket, spatial precision created another failure mode. The agent gets only three guesses, and each one has to get both the interaction type and the 3D location right. During evaluation, a prediction has to fall within **2.5 Å** of a compatible hidden point, and each hidden point can match at most one prediction. So clustering three good guesses around the same hotspot still only gives you one correct answer.

There was also a more general interface problem. The original harness exposed the same generic drug-design tools across tasks, even though IPD only required the agent to reason about three 3D points and submit them in a strict CSV format.<Sidenote number="3" align="paragraph">The original harness requires the agent to manually construct `solution.csv`, save it correctly, and then submit its file path. Agents sometimes fumbled the file creation or formatting step. In `smdd_002_Q92769_0`, the model produced the intended three points but separated the values with spaces instead of commas, which left the evaluator unable to parse the CSV.</Sidenote> This added unnecessary execution burden around tool choice, formatting, and submission that was separate from the core scientific problem itself.

These failures were not all failures of scientific reasoning. Some arose because the model was operating with an ambiguous representation of the task itself. That gave us an obvious first question for harness engineering: how much performance could we recover simply by making the intended problem easier to perceive and execute correctly?

### Fixing the representations

The first set of failures gave us an easy place to start. We made it explicit that the model had to predict ligand-side interaction points in the pocket, rather than receptor atoms themselves. We also removed irrelevant tools<Sidenote number="4" align="paragraph">The original harness exposed the same generic drug-design interface across tasks, including tools such as ADMET prediction and generic file-based submission. For IPD, most of this interface was unnecessary: Boltz was later reintroduced deliberately as a way for the agent to generate its own ligand-side evidence.</Sidenote> and replaced manual CSV formatting with a dedicated submission interface which directly accepts the 3 points from the model instead of needing a CSV file.

These changes removed some of the execution difficulty, but they did not solve the central problem we saw in the trajectories. Even when the model identified chemically sensible regions of the pocket, it still had little evidence for deciding which of those interactions would actually be observed across different binding molecules.

So we made one more change to the harness. Instead of having the model infer conservation entirely from a single receptor structure, we gave it a way to perform small computational experiments. The agent could design probe molecules, use Boltz to predict how they might bind, and inspect the ligand-side pharmacophore features in the resulting poses. By comparing several probes, it could construct a crude ligand ensemble of its own and look for interaction regions that appeared repeatedly.

The goal of these changes was not to dictate how the agent should solve the task, but to remove avoidable execution and representation burdens so it could focus on decisions that actually required scientific reasoning.

<IpdHarnessFigure />

There was a substantial improvement in Qwen's performance, but it also changed the kind of failure we were looking at. Once the agent could recover plausible ligand-side evidence more reliably, the remaining question was whether it knew how to generate useful probes and how much trust to place in the evidence they produced.

### What is still going wrong?

The improvement by itself does not mean that the probing strategy was particularly reliable. By allowing the model to generate synthetic probe molecules, the challenge shifted from understanding the pocket to choosing useful probes and making sense of the evidence they produced.

The first problem is the probes themselves. They are generated entirely by the model after inspecting the pocket, but we do not yet know what actually drives these choices. A probe might reflect a targeted hypothesis about a particular interaction region, a generic molecule suggested by the model's chemical priors, or target-specific chemistry recalled from pre-training or post-training. In some trajectories, the probes were also highly similar to one another, meaning that several apparently independent observations could really be repeated measurements of almost the same hypothesis.

The second problem is deciding how much to trust the resulting evidence. Boltz returns a binding probability alongside each predicted pose, but the current harness does not use that confidence when aggregating pharmacophore features. A feature extracted from a weakly supported pose therefore contributes just as much evidence as one from a much more credible predicted binder. As the figure below shows, most probes had fairly low binding probabilities, and seven trajectories appeared to over-trust noisy probe features.

<IpdProbeConfidence />

As a result, if several weak poses happen to place the same feature in a similar region, the agent can easily mistake repeated model error for conserved ligand evidence.

We also observed basic search-control failures. Eighteen of the 25 Qwen trajectories submitted with unused Boltz budget, leaving 54 of 200 available calls unused. Additional probes using these spare calls could have helped resolve ambiguous hotspots. Even after gathering probe evidence, three trajectories submitted two predictions that could match only the same hidden interaction point. Because the evaluator uses one-to-one matching, only one prediction in each pair could receive credit.

At this point, further harness engineering becomes harder to distinguish from writing the scientific policy for the agent by hand. We could add confidence weighting, enforce probe diversity, push the model to use more of its Boltz budget, or prevent competing predictions around the same hotspot. But each additional rule makes more of the experimental strategy ours rather than the model's. Ideally, the model should be discovering new ways to solve the scientific problem, rather than us progressively encoding those methods into the harness.

## Lead Optimization

IPD showed how representation bottlenecks alone can limit harness performance. The model struggled largely because essential information was difficult to represent and reason over, and fixing that representation took us quite far. However, not every task failed for the same reason.

Lead Optimization (LO) presented the opposite challenge. Here, the model frequently proposed reasonable chemistry on its own. The real difficulty was tracking state after proposing those molecules.

In an LO task, the agent starts with a protein-bound reference molecule and modifies it to optimize specific properties while keeping others within target thresholds. The candidate must preserve target binding, satisfy hard chemical constraints, and maintain sufficient Tanimoto similarity to the reference. To guide this search, the agent uses RDKit for validity and similarity checks, ADMET-AI for property predictions like toxicity and permeability, and Boltz to evaluate binding pose and affinity. Because expensive ADMET and Boltz evaluations operate under strict budgets, the agent must carefully prioritize which candidates to test.

### How well do current agents do?

Current models handle Lead Optimization much better than Interaction Point Discovery. Generating valid molecules is rarely the main issue. The real challenge is improving target properties without breaking existing ones, all while maintaining strong binding throughout the search. Although a single pass or fail score flattens these complex trade-offs, it provides a clear baseline to see how much performance the harness can help recover.

<LoBaselineFigure />

The real difference occurs at the Boltz binding stage. GPT-5.4 and Gemini 3.1 Pro achieve an 89.7% pass rate, while Qwen 3.5 9B reaches only 54.4%. Claude offers a notable contrast, maintaining strong performance through pre-Boltz checks but losing final task completions due to a lower binding pass rate.

This suggests that proposing a plausible candidate is only part of the problem. The agent still has to carry the right molecule and its associated evidence through the rest of the optimization process.

The search itself can be long and state-heavy. LO runs can stretch across dozens of turns, with some trajectories continuing for 75 turns or more. Over that time, an agent might propose multiple analogues, evaluate disjoint property subsets, discover marginal constraint violations, identify promising binding poses, and circle back to earlier candidates much later in the trajectory. Across these steps, it must continuously track which measurement belongs to which molecule, which properties remain unmeasured, which constraints are satisfied, and how much oracle budget remains.

<LoCandidateStory />

In the original harness, the model had to track all of this information entirely within its own context. The environment provided no centralized registry for explored candidates or their associated evidence. The trajectory above highlights how messy this tracking becomes. One edit can fix a constraint while exposing another, and the model must carry those changing measurements forward across many later turns. While we initially expected molecular design to be the primary hurdle, analyzing failed trajectories showed that state tracking was a major source of failure.

### What are the agents getting wrong?

One of the most common failures was surprisingly simple. The model would propose a promising molecule, test it correctly, discover that it failed a key requirement, and then submit that same molecule anyway. Even though the negative result was recorded earlier in the run, the agent failed to use that evidence when making its final decision.

Tracking individual candidates also degraded over longer trajectories. When testing multiple related analogues across disjoint property evaluations, the agent had no structured system to link measurements to specific structures. This led to cross-contamination of results between molecules, overlooked property gaps, and lost progress when circling back to previous designs.

<LoFailureMap />

Agents also struggled to parse and maintain the optimization constraints. Because different tasks use directional bounds or tight tolerances around the starting molecule, models often mixed up the required direction of change. In several cases, the agent misread a one-directional target as a two-sided band or re-calculated the target threshold mid-search, resulting in contradictory goals within the same trajectory.

Even when the search found a strong candidate, the final verification step frequently failed. A model would spend turns optimizing a molecule, introduce a minor last-minute edit, and submit the new structure without re-evaluating every constraint. Non-deterministic Boltz calls compounded the issue, leading agents to mistake a single borderline prediction for robust evidence.

What stood out across these failures was that chemistry was rarely the main bottleneck. The trajectories often contained useful candidates and sensible modifications. The true challenge was maintaining a coherent picture of the search over time, including what had been tried, which tests each molecule had passed, how reliable those measurements were, and what still required validation before submission.

### Fixing the state

The failures pointed towards a fairly simple division of responsibility. We still wanted the model to decide which molecules to make, which experiments were worth running, and what to do with the results. But there was little reason for it to also remember every threshold, track which measurement belonged to which analogue, or reconstruct the evidence for its finalist from dozens of earlier turns.

So we started by moving this state out of the trajectory and into the harness directly.

First, the harness locked in the task requirements as an immutable specification with clear thresholds and optimization goals. It then assigned every unique, canonicalized molecule a persistent ID and stored its measurements in a central ledger. Routine validity checks were handled natively, constraint margins were calculated automatically, and external oracle calls were tied to the specific candidate they evaluated.

<LoStateHarnessFigure />

Long trajectories created a second problem. Even with the important state stored explicitly, the conversational history itself could grow large enough that earlier reasoning and failed approaches became difficult to recover. Inspired by Agent Rosetta, once the context exceeded roughly 60K tokens, we asked the same model to compress the trajectory into a summary of what had been tried, what had been learned, and what it should consider doing next. We kept the two most recent actions verbatim so the local context was not lost, while the candidate ledger remained untouched and persisted in full.

This meant we could aggressively compact the model’s conversational history without compressing the scientific record itself. Older reasoning could be summarized, but candidate identities, measurements, failed gates, and remaining evidence stayed exact. This was particularly useful on the longer-running tasks, where the agent could otherwise spend much of its context repeatedly reconstructing decisions it had already made.

Importantly, the harness did not suggest which molecule to test next or otherwise influence the scientific search. It simply made the current state explicit. At any point, the agent could inspect a candidate together with its measurements, unmet constraints, and remaining budget, while older conversational history could be compacted without losing that authoritative state.

This also changed how final submissions worked. Under the original setup, the molecule written to the `solution.smi` file could differ from the one actually tested, or carry a known failing score from an earlier turn. The new harness required submissions to reference a registered candidate directly and accepted a normal submission only when that exact structure had complete passing evidence. The agent could still force an incomplete submission as a fallback, but that became a deliberate choice rather than an accidental state-tracking error.

The intervention did not change how the model searched. It simply made the evidence it had already gathered much harder to lose, misattribute, or misread.

### Did better state management help?

For Qwen 3.5 9B, the impact was substantial. Across the benchmark of 68 Lead Optimization tasks, fully passing solutions rose from 13 to 33, increasing the overall success rate from 19.1% to 48.5%.

What makes this result more interesting is that V2 did not get there by submitting more often. The original harness produced an answer on 66 of the 68 tasks, but only 13 of those submissions passed every requirement. Under V2, the agent submitted on just 48 tasks. Of those, 33 were normal submissions with complete passing evidence, while 9 were forced submissions with known failures and another 6 were missing required evidence. The remaining runs reached the turn limit before submitting.

The harness made the agent considerably more selective. Among submitted answers, the passing rate rose from 19.7% to 68.8%. Because the original harness did not categorize incomplete or forced attempts prior to submission, the cleaner evaluation remains the task-level outcome, rising from 13/68 to 33/68 for an improvement of 29.4 percentage points.

The gain also did not come from simply letting the model reason for longer. Across the full 68-task run, V2 used 12% fewer turns, 26% fewer tokens, and 17% fewer ADMET calls. Boltz usage moved in the opposite direction, increasing by 51%. With cheap constraints and candidate state handled explicitly, the model spent fewer turns and tokens overall while making substantially more use of Boltz, shifting more of its oracle budget towards checking whether promising candidates actually bound.

<LoHarnessOutcomes />

There was still a clear limit. Twenty V2 trajectories reached the turn limit without submitting, compared to only two under V1. While explicit state management removed many avoidable bookkeeping errors, it could not guide the agent out of an unproductive search path or suggest better chemical modifications. These remaining roadblocks pointed directly to limitations in the search policy itself.

Lead Optimization showed that a large fraction of failures could be recovered by making the agent’s state explicit and reliable. The next question was whether the same approach would help when the bottleneck was less about remembering the search and more about reasoning over the molecule itself.

## Scaffold Hopping

The difficulty is that these two goals pull in opposite directions. A scaffold hop must move far enough in chemical space to count as a genuinely different molecular solution, while preserving the interactions that made the original ligand work in the first place.

<LoVsScaffoldHopFigure />

In other words, Lead Optimization rewards controlled local change; Scaffold Hopping asks the agent to change the molecular representation while preserving its function inside the pocket.

### How well do current agents do?

Almost not at all. Across the 52 Scaffold Hopping tasks, no evaluated model solves more than two. Claude Sonnet 4.6, DeepSeek V3.2, GPT-5.4, and an additional Qwen 3.5 9B baseline each solve 2/52, while the remaining models solve one task or none.

This is a sharp contrast with Lead Optimization, where current models can solve a substantial fraction of the benchmark. Here, even the best strict pass rate is only 3.8%.

<ScaffoldHopBaselineFigure />

The few successes are not simply the same easy tasks being rediscovered by every model. Across all eight models, nine different tasks are solved at least once, with different models occasionally finding different successful scaffold hops. The problem is therefore not confined to one universally impossible subset. Instead, almost every model appears to be running into the same broader bottleneck. The near-floor aggregate leaves open whether agents cannot propose viable scaffold changes at all, or whether useful candidates are being lost elsewhere in the trajectory.

### What are the agents getting wrong?

The terminal scores suggest that Scaffold Hopping is impossible across the board, but breaking down the specific evaluation checks reveals a clearer pattern.

Generating a structurally distinct molecule is rarely the bottleneck. Models consistently clear the Tanimoto and scaffold-novelty thresholds across most tasks, and several regularly propose structures with high predicted binding probability.

The systematic failure lies in preserving the reference molecule’s binding pattern inside the pocket. No model manages this on more than 3 of 52 tasks. GPT-5.4 clears the binding-probability threshold on 46 tasks, yet reproduces the required pocket interactions on only 3. Claude satisfies all scaffold-novelty rules across all 52 tasks and clears binding on 48, but preserves the reference interaction pattern on just 2.

This highlights the true challenge. Agents can readily generate novel chemistry that still appears capable of binding to the target. The hard part is designing a different scaffold that binds in the same way as the original molecule.

### Exposing binding geometry to the agent

The issue was rooted in how the original harness returned results. Boltz put the binding score directly in front of the model, while the evidence needed to verify the binding mode remained buried in generated structure files. Once an agent found a sufficiently novel molecule with a high binding probability, it was easy to treat the search as essentially complete.

But binding strongly is not the same as binding in the same way. Verifying a scaffold hop required the agent to inspect the reference complex, locate the Boltz structure files, extract key protein-ligand interactions, and compare them against the predicted candidate poses. In practice, models rarely carried this full comparison through.

We therefore redesigned the interface around making this structural evidence directly accessible. As in Lead Optimization, every proposed molecule was canonicalized, registered under a persistent candidate ID, and passed through deterministic novelty checks before spending an expensive Boltz call. The protein and pocket configuration were also bound directly to the task.

The more important change came after Boltz. In the original harness, the response returned a binding probability together with the directory containing the generated structures. In V2, each result was attached directly to the candidate and returned the predicted pose files explicitly.<Sidenote number="5" align="paragraph"><BoltzInterfaceNote /></Sidenote> The agent could now move directly from asking whether a molecule appears to bind to inspecting how it binds.

The harness still did not calculate the scaffold-hopping interaction score or tell the model which contacts needed to survive. Deciding whether a candidate preserved the important interactions, how to modify it when it did not, and whether another experiment was worthwhile remained part of the model's scientific reasoning. We also removed irrelevant tools and tied final submission to the same registered candidate state.

This gave us a cleaner test of the remaining question: once the agent had direct visibility into both scaffold novelty and pocket geometry, would Scaffold Hopping success rates finally improve?

### Did exposing the binding geometry help?

The new harness fixed execution, yet the underlying scientific challenge remained difficult to solve.

Under the original harness, Qwen 3.5 9B submitted answers on only 39 of 52 tasks, with 13 trajectories exhausting their turn limit. The updated harness increased completion significantly, reaching submission on 51 of 52 tasks and yielding 50 valid evaluations. It also became far more reliable at satisfying earlier task requirements, lifting scaffold-novelty passes from 36 to 50 and doubling the candidates that cleared the binding-probability threshold from 14 to 28.

Yet final performance remained completely stagnant. Fully resolved tasks did not improve, shifting marginally from 2/52 down to 1/52.

<ScaffoldHopHarnessOutcomes />

The reason becomes clear at the final evaluation step. While the updated V2 harness produced more novel molecules and far more candidates with high predicted binding, it failed to improve preservation of the reference binding pattern. Only one candidate crossed that threshold, compared with three under the original harness. In fact, the two setups did not even solve the same tasks. The V2 harness found one novel solution while losing both tasks solved by the original baseline.

This outcome stands in stark contrast to Lead Optimization. Fixing operational and state-management flaws previously unlocked major performance gains. Here, making 3D poses accessible simply cleaned up the workspace around an unsolved scientific bottleneck. Seeing the binding geometry did not help the model determine which contacts were essential or how to modify the core to retain them.

The redesigned harness was also substantially cheaper to run. It used roughly 40% fewer tokens and 22% fewer turns, while reaching twelve more submissions and using slightly fewer Boltz calls. So the failure was not caused by giving the model less opportunity to search. If anything, V2 made the search cleaner and more efficient while leaving the same underlying difficulty exposed.

At this point, further manual intervention would again start to cross the line we were trying to preserve. We could explicitly extract and compare pocket interactions for the model, rank candidates by how closely they reproduce the reference pose, or suggest structural edits that restore lost contacts. But those are increasingly decisions about how to solve Scaffold Hopping, rather than how to make the task easier for an agent to execute.

Scaffold Hopping shows an important limit of this process. Cleaning up the interface can make the agent more reliable, more efficient, and much clearer about what evidence it has without necessarily improving the final success rate. Once those avoidable failures are removed, the remaining bottleneck can simply move closer to the scientific decision itself. In this case, exposing the binding geometry made the problem easier to see and act on, but it did not teach the model how to design a new scaffold that preserved the right interactions.

## What was the human doing all this time?

When you look back, the final interventions look pretty obvious. Interaction Point Discovery pushed us to expose better ligand-side evidence which is easier for the model to understand. Lead Optimization pushed us to externalize the persistent state of candidate molecules. Scaffold Hopping pushed us to surface the predicted binding geometry that had previously been difficult for the model to inspect.

Getting to those conclusions was much less obvious.

Each iteration to improve the model's performance was essentially from the same core manual loop: run agents, read failed trajectories, work out whether the failure came from the model's lack of scientific ability or the interface around it, change the harness, rerun the benchmark, and then inspect what went wrong. Across the three tasks, we manually audited hundreds of trajectories, often following individual tool calls and candidate histories across dozens of turns before a consistent failure pattern became visible.

Across the three tasks, this process took roughly **a month and a half** of manual iteration. The final changes were often relatively small. Most of the time went into figuring out which failures actually belonged to the harness, testing a fix, rerunning the benchmark, and then working out what had broken next.

More importantly, the useful intervention was different each time. In IPD, we eventually realised that the model was being asked to infer an ensemble property from a representation that exposed almost none of the relevant ligand-side evidence. In Lead Optimization, the chemistry was often good enough and the real problem was that the candidate state degraded over long trajectories. In Scaffold Hopping, the necessary structural information technically existed, but the interface made a scalar binding score much easier to use than the predicted poses needed to verify the binding mode.

None of these fixes came from a general rule like "add more tools" or "write a better prompt". The difficult part was deciding **what kind of failure we were looking at, what information the harness should make explicit, and where to stop before the harness started solving the scientific problem directly instead of letting the agent be the one solving it.**

And after every redesign, the answer could still be wrong. Lead Optimization improved dramatically. IPD improved but exposed a deeper probe-selection problem. Scaffold Hopping became cleaner and more efficient without improving terminal success. Manual harness engineering was therefore not just implementation work. It was an iterative search over possible representations, interfaces, and model–environment boundaries.

**If harness engineering is itself a search process, how much of that search can we automate?**

## Can the harness engineer itself?

There are already several ways to automate parts of harness engineering, including prompt optimizers such as GEPA. But our manual interventions touched much more than the prompt: tools, state representation, compaction, oracle wrappers, and submission logic all changed across the three tasks. We therefore wanted a method where the **entire executable harness** could be part of the search space.

For this, we used **Meta-Harness**, which treats harness design itself as an iterative optimization problem.

<MetaHarnessOverviewFigure />

The loop is straightforward. A meta-agent sees the source code, execution traces, and evaluation scores from previous harnesses, proposes a new version, evaluates it on the task, stores the resulting traces and score, and repeats. Because the harness itself is executable code, the optimizer is free to change much more than instructions alone.

We initialized this search from the same baseline harness used in our manual experiments and asked whether automated search could recover the kinds of improvements we had spent weeks finding by hand.

There is one boundary we kept fixed. The optimizer could redesign how public task information was represented and how the agent interacted with its environment, but it could not expose hidden evaluator answers or directly encode task-specific solutions. The scientific policy still had to belong to the child agent.

There was also a more personal reason I found this setup interesting. Throughout the manual phase, Claude Opus 5 had already been my main workhorse for reading trajectories, organizing failure modes, and brainstorming possible harness changes. I was still choosing which hypotheses to pursue, deciding where the model–harness boundary should sit, and implementing the final interventions, but a capable model was already involved in much of the analysis.

Meta-Harness therefore made the comparison more direct. If essentially the same kind of model could inspect the traces, reason about what was going wrong, edit the harness itself, and evaluate the result, **how much was I actually contributing to the loop?** Was the human mainly providing taste and judgment that the model could not recover on its own, or was I just manually orchestrating a process that could already be automated?

### How did we run the search?

To make the comparison fair, we kept the downstream scientific agent fixed. Qwen 3.5 9B served as the model solving the SMDD tasks, while Claude Opus acted as the meta-agent optimizing the harness. This setup created a direct test against the human baseline: Opus had already been my primary tool for analyzing trajectories and brainstorming manual fixes. The question was whether the meta-agent still needed a human in the loop to translate diagnoses into working code.

For each task family, the automated search started from the same original harness rather than from our manually improved version. The meta-agent could inspect the harness source code, trajectories produced by previous candidates, and their evaluation results, then propose a new executable harness for the next round.

We placed very few restrictions on how it could improve that harness. Prompts were editable, but so were the tools themselves, how state was represented, what information tool calls returned, how context was managed, and the control logic around submission. In other words, the search space included essentially everything around the child model that we had spent the previous sections modifying by hand.

What remained untouched was equally important. The child model was fixed, the underlying SMDD tasks and scientific oracles stayed constant, and the optimizer received no hidden answers or task-specific shortcuts. A candidate harness earned reward only by helping Qwen solve the task directly.

We ran this search independently for each of the three task families. Each search used 20 optimization tasks, with four rollouts per task, giving 80 trajectories for every candidate harness. Starting from the base harness, Meta-Harness then proposed 10 additional iterations, so each task family required 11 harness evaluations × 80 trajectories = 880 trajectories. Across all three task families, the automated search therefore generated 2,640 child-agent trajectories.

We deliberately kept the optimization signal simple: every Meta-Harness run was scored only by **terminal task success**. We did not construct task-specific reward rubrics or shape the objective using the failure modes we had discovered manually. That made the comparison cleaner—the automated optimizer received the same definition of success across tasks, rather than an additional layer of human engineering in the reward itself.

This was a deliberately conservative choice for studying harness search, not necessarily the reward we would choose if the only goal were maximum benchmark performance.

## Human taste vs automated search

The automated optimizer started with the same raw ingredients as the human process (code, trajectories, error traces, and iterative attempts) while receiving none of our explicit diagnoses about what to fix.

So, **which harness worked better?**

**Manual engineering came out ahead overall, but not on every task.**

<HumanVsAHEFigure />

The largest advantage was in Lead Optimization. Manual V2 solved 33 of 68 tasks, compared with 13 under both the original harness and automated search. On the paired comparison, V2 uniquely solved 26 tasks while AHE uniquely solved six. The automated optimizer had therefore failed to produce a meaningful improvement over the starting harness, while the manual redesign recovered a large fraction of the benchmark.

Interaction Point Discovery showed the same direction, although the signal was better expressed through partial credit. The original harness recovered 10 of 75 hidden points, automated search recovered 12, and Manual V2 recovered 30. V2 improved the point count on 16 of the 25 paired tasks and regressed on only three. Complete solutions remained rare (two under V2 and none under AHE), so the stronger result here is not the terminal solve count, but the much broader recovery of correct interaction points.

Scaffold Hopping was the exception. Automated harness search produced the strongest observed Scaffold Hopping result, reaching 5/52 compared with 2/52 for V1 and 1/52 for Manual V2. The absolute numbers are small, and the paired terminal difference is not statistically decisive. The direction remains revealing nonetheless, as this was the only task family where automated search found a harness that improved over both the starting point and our manual redesign.

The more useful distinction was what kind of change helped. Our largest gains came from fixing representation and state, while Scaffold Hopping improved when automated search changed the search strategy itself.

### What did the automated optimizer actually learn?

Across all three tasks, Claude did not converge on one generic recipe for making Qwen more reliable. Each run developed its own sequence of hypotheses, reacting to failures in the previous harness and gradually changing both the workflow and the instructions given to the child model.

Lead Optimization was the clearest case where automated search converged toward many of the same problems we had identified manually. The base harness left almost all of the bookkeeping inside the trajectory. Qwen had to remember the task requirements, keep track of which measurements belonged to which molecule, decide what was still missing, and make sure the final submission corresponded to the candidate it had actually evaluated.

Meta-Harness started attacking this quickly. By iteration 1, it had introduced a staged cheap-to-expensive workflow, a running candidate table, persistent memory for candidates and hypotheses, budget checkpoints, and explicit instructions to measure candidates before moving on. This made trajectories considerably more structured and shorter, although it did not yet improve correctness.

The next iteration made the procedure progressively harder to violate. Iteration 2 stopped Qwen from freely typing candidate SMILES, required candidates to be generated through RDKit transformations, required objective measurements before submission, and added an explicit candidate-level gate table. It also verified that the exact molecule written to the output file was the one that had been measured. This became the highest-scoring harness observed during the automated search and was the version we retained for the final benchmark comparison.

Claude later went even further. By iteration 4, every requirement was explicitly routed to its intended measurement source, unknown gates had to be measured before submission, known failures were supposed to keep the search running, and fully passing candidates were ranked by their weakest margin. In other words, the optimizer had independently arrived at much of the same high-level diagnosis: Lead Optimization was failing because evidence from different candidates and different gates was not being integrated reliably at the final decision.

<AheVsHumanStateFigure />

The search dynamics were stepwise and noisy rather than monotonic. Most of the gain arrived with the iteration-2 finalist and submission checks. Later harnesses sometimes solved a broader set of tasks, but none surpassed that iteration's trajectory-level reward.

Meta-Harness gave Qwen a progressively better procedure for maintaining state. It also introduced persistent memory and control machinery, so this was more than simply rewriting the prompt. But candidate identity, requirement completion, and final selection still ultimately depended on Qwen applying that machinery correctly.

Our Manual V2 redesign moved those responsibilities one level further out. The task specification was compiled once and made immutable. Every molecule received a canonical candidate ID. ADMET and Boltz results were attached to that ID in an append-only ledger rather than reconstructed from conversation history. Gate status and signed margins were computed from that stored evidence, and a normal submission was rejected unless every required gate was known and passing. Compaction could summarize the trajectory, but it could not rewrite the candidate state underneath it.

That distinction is subtle, but important. Both approaches recognized the bookkeeping problem. Automated search made the bookkeeping easier for Qwen to perform, while Manual V2 made less of the bookkeeping Qwen's responsibility in the first place.

The automated search was also noisy. The chosen iteration achieved 37.5% in its initial 80-rollout evaluation and 27.5% upon re-evaluation, while another duplicate run shifted from 33.75% to 28.75%. The optimization trajectory was therefore noisy rather than monotonic, with substantial variance even between identical harnesses. Some of that instability is likely exacerbated by the sparsity of the terminal reward, especially on tasks where complete success is rare.

IPD diverged more sharply. Unlike Lead Optimization, the automated optimizer did not converge on the same abstraction we had chosen manually. Instead, it kept trying to make Qwen better at inferring the hidden pharmacophore directly from receptor geometry.

Early changes focused on execution hygiene and task management. Iteration 2 stripped down an overcomplicated tool flow, guided Qwen to log a tentative answer early, and tightened turn-budget tracking. Iteration 3 replaced isolated hotspot rankings with whole-triplet scoring, enforcing limits on centroid location, inter-point distances, and spatial spread. By iteration 7, Claude introduced burial-weighted PCA to identify the main cavity axis and disperse the three points across a ligand-sized envelope. This setup achieved the best partial recovery in the automated run, identifying 43 of 240 points across 80 rollouts, while zero-shot complete solutions stayed rare.

There is an important asymmetry in what the optimizer could learn from these runs. Meta-Harness only received binary terminal success as its reward. In IPD, a trajectory recovering two of the three hidden points received the same reward as one recovering none: zero. The partial-point metrics we use here to understand the search trajectory were diagnostic measurements computed afterward, not optimization signal available to Claude.

<IpdAheVsHumanFigure />

That choice was intentional. Giving IPD a handcrafted partial-credit reward, Lead Optimization a gate-margin rubric, and Scaffold Hopping a weighted combination of novelty, binding, and interaction preservation would introduce another substantial piece of human design into the automated side of the comparison. But if the objective were simply to produce the strongest possible harness, richer rewards are an obvious direction. IPD in particular could expose matched-point recovery directly, while other tasks could use evaluator-derived rubrics or graded gate progress to provide much denser feedback.

So the automated search was clearly adapting to the task. It recognized that individually plausible interaction sites were not enough, that the three points had to form a coherent molecular-scale configuration, and that spatial coverage of the pocket mattered. But all of these interventions were still variations on the same basic strategy: infer the missing ligand geometry from the receptor.

Our manual redesign tackled the core representation directly. First, we made the output semantics explicit. Predictions represented ligand-side features, receptor donors mapped to ligand acceptors, repeated feature types were permitted, matching was strictly one-to-one, and predictions had to land within 2.5 Å of a target point. We also replaced manual file formatting with a structured pharmacophore submission interface.

The larger change was giving the model a way to generate its own ligand-side evidence. Qwen could propose probe molecules, predict their binding poses with Boltz, and extract aligned pharmacophore features such as Donors, Acceptors, Aromatics, Hydrophobics, Cations, and Anions. Across the final evaluation runs, this generated 138 successful unique probes and 694 ligand-feature records, with 23 of 25 tasks gathering evidence from multiple molecules. In contrast, the automated search generated zero valid molecular Boltz probes across its 880 optimization rollouts, even after probe generation appeared in later search iterations.

That distinction showed up clearly in the final paired evaluation. The selected automated harness recovered 12 of 75 hidden points, while Human V2 recovered 30. Tasks with at least one correct point rose from 9 to 20 out of 25, and tasks with at least two correct points grew from 3 to 8. Complete solutions remained rare (zero under AHE versus two under V2), but the shift in partial-credit recovery was unmistakable. Both configurations submitted on all 25 tasks, with V2 requiring fewer turns on average despite executing 146 Boltz predictions.

Automated search became increasingly sophisticated at reasoning from the receptor. Human V2 changed what the model was allowed to observe. Instead of asking Qwen to infer conserved ligand interactions from one protein structure, the harness gave it a crude ligand ensemble from which those interactions could be inferred.

<ScaffoldAheVsHumanFigure />

Scaffold Hopping reversed the pattern. Here, automated search produced the stronger harness, but not because it made the workflow cleaner in the same way as our manual redesign. The two approaches ended up putting pressure on different parts of the task.

The base harness made binding probability easy to observe, but preserving the reference binding mode was much harder. Candidate poses existed, yet the model still had to inspect them and determine whether key interactions remained intact. This created an obvious failure mode. The model would find a novel structure, observe a strong binding score, and terminate the search early despite disrupting the original contact network.

Meta-Harness gradually pushed against that behavior. Early iterations introduced task-bound candidate tools, cheap screening, and candidate memory. Later policies became more conservative about the chemical change itself, explicitly preserving anchors and linker geometry while changing the core, and requiring candidates to be measured before submission. The selected harness then reintroduced pose analysis after prediction and warned against drifting back toward the reference while optimizing binding. The result was not a monotonic improvement, but the best policies were clearly trying to balance novelty against interaction preservation rather than optimize either one in isolation.

Our manual V2 redesign went in a different direction. We made the deterministic parts of the task much harder to get wrong. Every candidate received a canonical identity, novelty was checked with evaluator-matched Tanimoto and scaffold-MCS calculations, malformed or non-novel candidates could be rejected before an expensive Boltz call, and predicted pose files were returned explicitly and attached to the tested candidate. The model no longer had to hunt through filesystem outputs just to inspect its own prediction.

The important limitation was what the harness could actually enforce. Normal submission required the exposed novelty and binding gates to pass, but the evaluator's interaction-similarity score remained hidden. Pose inspection and model-written interaction evidence were available, but they were advisory rather than a blocking gate. A force=false submission could therefore be completely valid according to the public harness state and still fail the benchmark because the binding interaction pattern had changed.

That is exactly what happened in the paired evaluation. Human V2 passed both novelty gates on all 50 evaluable submissions, compared with 39/45 for Tanimoto and 32/45 for scaffold MCS under AHE. But the interaction gate went the other way: 8/45 AHE submissions preserved the required interaction pattern, compared with only 1/50 under V2. Terminal success followed that difference, with AHE solving 5 of 52 tasks and Human V2 solving 1 of 52.

The result is therefore a useful limit case for manual harness engineering. We made novelty exact, candidate identity reliable, Boltz execution task-bound, and pose evidence easier to inspect, but none of those changes could make the hidden interaction requirement enforceable without moving further into the scientific policy itself. Automated search was willing to do exactly that: it increasingly shaped how Qwen should modify the molecule and what trade-offs it should preserve.

Across the three tasks, automated search was clearly capable of finding useful harness changes, but it did not simply rediscover the manual designs. In Lead Optimization it converged on similar bookkeeping failures but left more of the procedure in Qwen’s hands. In IPD it kept refining how Qwen reasoned from receptor geometry, while the manual harness changed the evidence available altogether. And in Scaffold Hopping, the direction flipped: the automated optimizer was willing to encode a stronger chemical search policy, and that produced the better harness. The remaining question, then, is less whether harness engineering can be automated and more where we want the harness to stop and the model’s scientific policy to begin.

## Conclusion

So, is human taste overrated in harness engineering?

Not entirely. But its value depends heavily on what part of the problem we are trying to optimize.

When the failure came from the way the task was represented to the model, what evidence it could access, or what state it was expected to maintain, the strongest gains came from human-designed abstractions. Lead Optimization improved when candidate identity and evidence stopped living inside the trajectory. Interaction Point Discovery improved when the harness stopped asking the model to infer conserved ligand behavior from a single receptor and instead exposed ligand-side evidence directly.

Automated search was much more competitive once the problem shifted from making the task legible to finding a better policy within an already usable interface. Scaffold Hopping was the clearest example. Our manual harness made novelty exact and pose evidence easier to inspect, but Meta-Harness was more willing to shape how Qwen searched the chemical space itself, and ultimately produced the stronger harness.

That suggests a useful division of labor. Human judgment still matters most when deciding what should be represented explicitly, what should be enforced by the environment, and what should remain the model’s responsibility. Once those choices are sensible, automated optimization can search over workflows, instructions, heuristics, and control policies far more cheaply than repeatedly doing the same loop by hand.

A natural stopping point exists. Interventions that maintain deterministic state or surface accessible task data belong to infrastructure. When the harness prescribes chemical transformations, prioritizes specific contacts, or negotiates scientific trade-offs, it crosses into policy design. Further progress is then better achieved by fine-tuning or training the model directly rather than hardcoding scientific reasoning into the harness.

So human taste is not disappearing from harness engineering. It is moving up a level. The interesting question is becoming less “What prompt or tool should I add?” and more “Where should the boundary between the environment and the scientist actually be?” Once that boundary is chosen well, much of the optimization inside it may not need a human at all.

## Final thoughts

SMDD-Bench has been useful precisely because many of its failures are difficult to classify cleanly as either “the model is bad at chemistry” or “the agent system is badly designed.” A model can propose chemically sensible candidates and still fail because it loses track of evidence; conversely, a perfectly organized harness can expose the remaining scientific bottleneck without actually solving it. That ambiguity makes the benchmark frustrating to work with, but also unusually useful for studying where scientific-agent capability actually lives.

There is still a lot we have not resolved. Interaction Point Discovery remains extremely sparse at full-task success, Scaffold Hopping still struggles to preserve binding interactions reliably, and several of the manual interventions described here have not yet been explored across every model or task family. We also only studied a subset of SMDD-Bench in depth. Other task families, including 2D Pharmacophore Identification and Fragment Assembly, may expose very different failure modes and may favor different kinds of harness intervention altogether. More broadly, the human-versus-automated patterns we observed here should be tested across a wider set of models, harnesses, task instances, and optimization procedures before treating them as general properties of scientific agents. Our experiments therefore probe only one part of a much larger space between changing the environment and changing the model. Once the harness has made the task legible and execution reliable, the obvious next step is to ask whether training the policy itself produces larger and more transferable gains than continuing to encode scientific strategy around it.

The optimization objective is another axis we have barely explored. We intentionally used binary terminal success throughout these experiments to avoid embedding our own task-specific diagnoses into the automated reward. That is useful for the comparison here, but probably leaves performance on the table. Future runs could test denser evaluator-derived signals, rubric-based rewards, or task-specific partial credit—such as the number of correctly recovered interaction points in IPD—and ask whether automated harness search becomes more competitive once it receives a better learning signal.
