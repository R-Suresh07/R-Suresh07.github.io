## Introduction

An agent harness is everything that surrounds the model: the tools it can call, the state it can see, and how its observations arrive. Solving a task with an agent therefore involves two decision procedures rather than one. While the model decides what to do with the information it receives, the harness decides what information reaches the model at all, including what gets remembered, what gets compacted away, and which actions it can even take.

The two are optimized very differently. The model's procedure is learned through training, while the harness's procedure is still commonly written by hand. It relies on a developer's "taste," built up from previous experience of what has gone wrong and what could go wrong in the future. This process is often cumbersome and time-consuming, since you have to run the loop of analyzing trajectories → analyzing failure modes → hypothesizing optimizations → rolling out, and then repeat.

This raises the question: why even bother? How much of what the harness does is work the model could do itself? We think the useful framing is not how much harness engineering can be automated, but how little is needed before the returns run out and further gains must come from the model’s policy. Ultimately, how far does harness engineering take you, and can we reach that limit without doing it by hand?

Our testing ground is the Small Molecule Drug Discovery (SMDD) Bench, a benchmark for challenging, multi-turn, long-horizon agentic tasks focused on small-molecule design. While LLMs have become very good at coding and math, which are easily and cheaply verifiable domains, generative chemistry provides a strong stress test. Generative chemistry is comparatively underexplored, and evaluating an answer is both expensive and imperfect.<Sidenote number="1">Boltz-2 lets us estimate whether a molecule is likely to bind to a protein without running a physical experiment. It's much cheaper than traditional simulation methods, but its predictions are still imperfect and can vary across protein targets.</Sidenote> A task instance is also more than just a text prompt: the agent receives a 3D protein structure, information about where the relevant binding pocket is, and, for some tasks, the 3D position of part of a molecule. None of this is naturally easy for a language model to interpret, so what the agent knows about the pocket depends heavily on what its tools compute and how that information is presented back to it.

## Inside an SMDD task

SMDD-Bench has five task types and 502 task instances in total. Each one tests chemical and biological reasoning, 3D intuition about the pocket, and fluency with specialized tools, usually all at once. We focus on three: Lead Optimization, Scaffold Hopping, and Interaction Point Discovery. Together, they span a wide range of constraints and leave a lot of room for improvement. Lead Optimization is the most manageable of the three, whereas current models still only solve a tiny fraction of Scaffold Hopping and Interaction Point Discovery tasks.<Sidenote number="2" align="paragraph"><SmddPerformanceNote /></Sidenote>

## Interaction Point Discovery

The goal of Interaction Point Discovery(IPD) is to test whether an agent can identify valuable interaction hotspots inside a protein binding pocket.

These hotspots are useful because they indicate regions that small molecules repeatedly exploit when binding to the protein. They can guide fragment-based drug discovery, suggest where an existing molecule should be elaborated, or help prioritize molecules during computational screening.

The agent is given a PDB structure of the protein together with the location of its binding pocket. Its job is to predict the three ligand-side interaction points most likely to be conserved across a diverse set of small-molecule binders.

For each point, the agent outputs its 3D coordinates and one of six pharmacophore labels: donor, acceptor, aromatic, hydrophobic, cation, or anion.

The difficulty in this task mainly comes from the fact that the agent is asked to predict a property that is not directly visible in it's input. It receives a single protein structure and the location of the binding pocket but the hidden points are constructed from interactions that occur many times across different ligand structures of the same protein. To put it simply, the agent can inspect what interactions like chemically plausible in one pocket but it has to reason which of those interactions would remain useful across a diverse set of binding molecules.

There is also a high degree of spatial reasoning involved in the task. The agent has only three predictions and each must identify both the correct interaction type and approximately where that feature would occur in 3D space. So the problem combines chemical reasoning, geometrical analysis and reasoning about information that is only indirectly observable from the provided protein receptor. 

### How well do current agents do?

Not great. Across the models evaluated in the original SMDD-Bench, only Gemini 3.1 Pro managed to solve a single IPD task, while every other model scored 0/25. Our additional Qwen 3.5 9B baseline also scored 0/25.

<IpdBaselineFigure />

These aggregate results show that current agents struggle on this task, but they do not reveal the root cause of those failures. To understand whether the models were struggling with the intended scientific problem or with more basic aspects of the task, we examined the failed trajectories in detail.

### What are the agents getting wrong?

One common strategy that the models used was to inspect the receptor.pdb, identify chemically plausible interaction sites around the pocket, and treat those as likely conserved hotspots. But **plausibility is not the same as conservation**. A binding pocket can contain many chemically attractive places for a ligand to interact, but what we actually care about is which of those possibilities are repeatedly used by different molecules binding to the same protein. This might sound like a small distinction, but it changes the problem considerably. A site can look perfectly reasonable from the receptor structure and still rarely recur across real binders.

A more surprising failure was that agents sometimes predicted the wrong side of the interaction altogether. The interaction points belong to the ligand, not the receptor itself. For example, if the protein exposes a hydrogen-bond donor, the target is not the donor atom itself, but the location where a ligand acceptor would sit. The agent therefore has to infer what complementary feature should occupy the empty pocket space based on the surrounding protein chemistry. This difference turned out to matter much more than we initially expected.

Even when agents identified sensible regions of the pocket, spatial precision created another failure mode. The agent gets only three guesses, and each one has to get both the interaction type and the 3D location right. During evaluation, a prediction has to fall within **2.5 Å** of a compatible hidden point, and each hidden point can match at most one prediction. So clustering three good guesses around the same hotspot still only gives you one correct answer.

There was also a more general interface problem. The original harness exposed the same generic drug-design tools across tasks, even though IPD only required the agent to reason about three 3D points and submit them in a strict CSV format.<Sidenote number="3" align="paragraph">The original harness requires the agent to manually construct `solution.csv`, save it correctly, and then submit its file path. Agents sometimes fumbled the file creation or formatting step. In `smdd_002_Q92769_0`, the model produced the intended three points but separated the values with spaces instead of commas, which left the evaluator unable to parse the CSV.</Sidenote> This added unnecessary execution burden around tool choice, formatting, and submission that was separate from the core scientific problem itself.

These failures were not all failures of scientific reasoning. Some arose because the model was operating with an ambiguous representation of the task itself. That gave us an obvious first question for harness engineering: how much performance could we recover simply by making the intended problem easier to perceive and execute correctly?

### Fixing the representations

The first set of failures gave us an easy place to start. We made it explicit that the model had to predict ligand-side interaction points in the pocket, rather than receptor atoms themselves. We also removed irrelevant tools<Sidenote number="4" align="paragraph">The original harness exposed the same generic drug-design interface across tasks, including tools such as ADMET prediction and generic file-based submission. For IPD, most of this interface was unnecessary: Boltz was later reintroduced deliberately as a way for the agent to generate its own ligand-side evidence.</Sidenote> and replaced manual CSV formatting with a dedicated submission interface which directly accepts the 3 points from the model instead of needing a CSV file.

These changes removed some of the execution difficulty, but they did not solve the central problem we saw in the trajectories. Even when the model identified chemically sensible regions of the pocket, it still had little evidence for deciding which of those interactions would actually be observed across different binding molecules.

So we made one more change to the harness. Instead of having the model infer conservation entirely from a single receptor structure, we gave it a way to perform small computational experiments. The agent could design probe molecules, use Boltz to predict how they might bind, and inspect the ligand-side pharmacophore features in the resulting poses. By comparing several probes, it could construct a crude ligand ensemble of its own and look for interaction regions that appeared repeatedly.

The goal of these changes was not to dictate how the agent should solve the task, but to remove avoidable execution and representation burdens so it could focus on decisions that actually required scientific reasoning.

<IpdHarnessFigure />

There was a substantial improvement in Qwen's performance, but it also changed the kind of failure we were looking at. Once the agent could recover plausible ligand-side evidence more reliably, the remaining question was whether it knew how to generate useful probes and how much trust to place in the evidence they produced.

### What is still going wrong?

The improvement by itself does not mean that the probing strategy was particularly reliable. By allowing the model to generate synthetic probe molecules, the challenge shifted from understanding the pocket to choosing useful probes and making sense of the evidence they produced.

The first problem is the probes themselves. They are generated entirely by the model after inspecting the pocket, but we do not yet know what actually drives these choices. A probe might reflect a targeted hypothesis about a particular interaction region, a generic molecule suggested by the model's chemical priors, or target-specific chemistry recalled from pre-training or post-training. In some trajectories, the probes were also highly similar to one another, meaning that several apparently independent observations could really be repeated measurements of almost the same hypothesis.

The second problem is deciding how much to trust the resulting evidence. Boltz returns a binding probability alongside each predicted pose, but the current harness does not use that confidence when aggregating pharmacophore features. A feature extracted from a weakly supported pose therefore contributes just as much evidence as one from a much more credible predicted binder. As the figure below shows, most probes had fairly low binding probabilities, and seven trajectories appeared to over-trust noisy probe features.

<IpdProbeConfidence />

As a result, if several weak poses happen to place the same feature in a similar region, the agent can easily mistake repeated model error for conserved ligand evidence.

We also observed basic search-control failures. Eighteen of the 25 Qwen trajectories submitted with unused Boltz budget, leaving 54 of 200 available calls unused. Additional probes using these spare calls could have helped resolve ambiguous hotspots. Even after gathering probe evidence, three trajectories submitted two predictions that could match only the same hidden interaction point. Because the evaluator uses one-to-one matching, only one prediction in each pair could receive credit.

At this point, further harness engineering becomes harder to distinguish from writing the scientific policy for the agent by hand. We could add confidence weighting, enforce probe diversity, push the model to use more of its Boltz budget, or prevent competing predictions around the same hotspot. But each additional rule makes more of the experimental strategy ours rather than the model's. Ideally, the model should be discovering new ways to solve the scientific problem, rather than us progressively encoding those methods into the harness.

## Lead Optimization

IPD showed how representation bottlenecks alone can limit harness performance. The model struggled largely because essential information was difficult to represent and reason over, and fixing that representation took us quite far. However, not every task failed for the same reason.

Lead Optimization(LO) presented the opposite challenge. Here, the model frequently proposed reasonable chemistry on its own. The real difficulty was tracking state after proposing those molecules.

In an LO task, the agent starts with a protein-bound reference molecule and modifies it to optimize specific properties while keeping others within target thresholds. The candidate must preserve target binding, satisfy hard chemical constraints, and maintain sufficient Tanimoto similarity to the reference. To guide this search, the agent uses RDKit for validity and similarity checks, ADMET-AI for property predictions like toxicity and permeability, and Boltz to evaluate binding pose and affinity. Because expensive ADMET and Boltz evaluations operate under strict budgets, the agent must carefully prioritize which candidates to test.

Consequently, a single trajectory accumulates substantial state very quickly. LO runs can stretch across dozens of turns, with some trajectories continuing for 75 turns or more. Over that time, an agent might propose multiple analogues, evaluate disjoint property subsets, discover marginal constraint violations, identify promising binding poses, and circle back to earlier candidates much later in the trajectory. Across these steps, it must continuously track which measurement belongs to which molecule, which properties remain unmeasured, which constraints are satisfied, and how much oracle budget remains.

In the original harness, the model had to track all of this information entirely within its own context. The environment provided no centralized registry for explored candidates or their associated evidence. While we initially expected molecular design to be the primary hurdle, analyzing failed trajectories showed that state tracking was the major reason for failures.
