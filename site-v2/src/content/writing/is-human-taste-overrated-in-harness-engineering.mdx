## Introduction

An agent harness is everything that surrounds the model: the tools it can call, the state it can see, and how observations are presented back to it. An agent therefore does not act on the environment directly. Its actions pass through an interface that decides what capabilities are available, while observations and results are transformed before they return to the model.

This means an agent system contains two decision procedures rather than one. The model decides what to do with the information it receives. The harness decides **what information reaches the model, what gets remembered, and which actions are possible in the first place.**

The asymmetry is that these two procedures are optimized very differently. The model’s policy is learned through training, while the harness around it is still commonly designed by hand. A developer decides which tools to expose, what state deserves a durable representation, how outputs should be structured, when context should be compacted, and what conditions should block an action or submission.

Those choices rely heavily on **human taste**. Usually, we run an agent, inspect where it failed, form a hypothesis about whether the problem came from the model or its environment, modify the harness, and run everything again. The final fix can be a few lines of code. Figuring out which few lines to change can take days.

This raises the question behind this post. **How far can harness engineering take us before the remaining bottleneck is the model’s scientific policy itself?** And if harness engineering is another iterative search process, how much of that search actually requires a human?

Our testing ground is the Small Molecule Drug Design benchmark, SMDD-Bench ([Han et al., 2026](#han-et-al-2026)), a collection of challenging, multi-turn agentic tasks in small-molecule design. Coding and math give agents relatively cheap and direct feedback when something goes wrong. Generative chemistry is far messier, because evaluating a candidate can require specialized scientific models, the feedback is imperfect, and several tasks depend on reasoning over 3D molecular structure.<Sidenote number="1">Boltz-2, which we shorten to Boltz below, lets us estimate whether a molecule is likely to bind to a protein without running a physical experiment. It is much cheaper than traditional simulation methods, but its predictions are still imperfect and can vary across protein targets.</Sidenote>

A task is also more than a text prompt. The agent may receive a 3D protein structure, the location of its binding pocket, or the 3D position of part of a molecule. None of this is naturally easy for a language model to interpret. **What the model knows about the scientific problem therefore depends heavily on what the harness computes, remembers, and chooses to show it.**

We focus on three SMDD-Bench tasks that expose different kinds of harness dependence. We first engineer their harnesses by hand, then ask whether an automated optimizer can discover similar improvements.

## Our testbed: SMDD-Bench

SMDD-Bench contains 502 instances across five small-molecule design tasks. Each combines chemical and biological reasoning with specialized tools, and several also require reasoning over the 3D geometry of a protein binding pocket.

We focus on Interaction Point Discovery, Lead Optimization, and Scaffold Hopping. They sit at very different points on the current capability curve. Lead Optimization is comparatively tractable, with the best reported model solving 57.6% of tasks, while the best reported pass rates for Scaffold Hopping and Interaction Point Discovery are only 3.8% and 4.0%, respectively.<Sidenote number="2" align="paragraph">These are the best reported pass rates in the original SMDD-Bench evaluation: GPT-5.4 for Lead Optimization, GPT-5.4 / Claude Sonnet 4.6 / DeepSeek V3.2 tied for Scaffold Hopping, and Gemini 3.1 Pro for Interaction Point Discovery.</Sidenote> This gives us one task where agents already succeed reasonably often and two where performance remains close to the floor.

## Interaction Point Discovery

The goal of Interaction Point Discovery (IPD) is to identify conserved interaction hotspots inside a protein binding pocket: regions where different molecules repeatedly make the same kinds of interactions when binding to the protein. These hotspots can then guide the design and optimization of new molecules.

The task contains a deliberate information asymmetry. **The agent receives a single protein receptor and the location of its binding pocket, but the hidden answer is derived from interactions recurring across many different bound ligands.** The agent must predict three ligand-side interaction points, specifying both their 3D coordinates and their **pharmacophore type** (the kind of chemical interaction the ligand feature can make, such as a hydrogen-bond donor, acceptor, or hydrophobic interaction).

This means the task is not simply asking which interactions look chemically plausible in the visible receptor. It asks which of those possibilities are likely to **recur across a diverse set of binders**. A chemically plausible interaction may therefore still be the wrong answer. And because the agent gets only three predictions, each one has to identify both the right interaction type and approximately the right location in 3D space.

### How well do current agents do?

Not great. Across the models evaluated in the original SMDD-Bench, only Gemini 3.1 Pro solved a single IPD task, while every other model scored **0/25**. Our additional Qwen 3.5 9B baseline also solved **0/25**.

But the all-or-nothing task metric hides substantial partial progress. **GPT-5.4, for example, recovered 17 of the 75 individual interaction points while solving no complete tasks.** Gemini recovered fewer points overall, 14/75, but was the only model to get all three points right on the same task.

<IpdBaselineFigure />

This gap between point-level recovery and full-task success suggests that the models are not simply failing everywhere. They can often identify part of the answer, but those partial successes rarely come together into a complete prediction. To understand what was preventing that, we inspected the failed trajectories directly.

### What are the agents getting wrong?

One common strategy was to inspect the receptor structure, identify chemically plausible interaction sites around the pocket, and treat those as likely conserved hotspots. **But plausibility is not the same as conservation.** A binding pocket can contain many reasonable places for a ligand to interact, while the task asks which of those interactions actually recur across different molecules binding to the same protein. A site can therefore look perfectly sensible from the receptor structure and still be a poor prediction of the hidden ensemble.

Agents also frequently reasoned about the wrong side of the interaction. The submitted points are supposed to describe **ligand-side features in empty pocket space**, not receptor atoms themselves. If the protein exposes a hydrogen-bond donor, for example, the relevant prediction is where a ligand acceptor would sit nearby. The model therefore has to reason about the **complementary feature a ligand would place in the pocket**, rather than simply identifying chemically interesting atoms on the protein.

Even when agents found sensible regions of the pocket, they could still waste their three predictions on the same interaction. The evaluator matches submitted points one-to-one against distinct hidden hotspots, so several nearby guesses around one region can still recover only a single point. With only three predictions available, the agent has to choose three distinct hypotheses and localize each one accurately.

There was also a simpler interface problem. The original harness exposed the same generic drug-design tools across tasks, even though IPD only required the agent to reason about three 3D points and submit them in a strict CSV format.<Sidenote number="3" align="paragraph">The original harness required the agent to manually construct `solution.csv` and submit its path. This occasionally failed for reasons unrelated to the scientific prediction itself. In `smdd_002_Q92769_0`, for example, the model produced three intended points but separated the values with spaces rather than commas, leaving the evaluator unable to parse the file.</Sidenote> This added tool-selection, file-writing, and formatting work that was separate from the scientific problem itself.

The important distinction is that these failures sit at different levels. **Inferring conservation from a single receptor is part of the scientific challenge. Confusing receptor atoms with ligand-side points, duplicating the same hotspot, or losing an otherwise valid answer to file formatting are not.** That gave us a natural place to start. We would remove the representation and execution failures first, then see what scientific difficulty remained.

### Fixing the representations

The first set of failures gave us a clear place to start. We refer to the original SMDD-Bench interface as V1, and the redesigned task-specific interface below as V2. We made it explicit that the model had to predict **ligand-side interaction points in the pocket**, rather than receptor atoms themselves. We also removed tools that were irrelevant to IPD<Sidenote number="4" align="paragraph">The original harness exposed the same generic drug-design interface across tasks, including ADMET property prediction and file-based submission. Most of these tools were irrelevant to IPD and added execution choices unrelated to predicting three interaction points.</Sidenote> and replaced manual CSV construction with a dedicated submission interface that accepts the three predicted points directly.

That removed some avoidable execution burden, but it did not solve the central problem we saw in the trajectories. Even when the model identified chemically sensible regions of the pocket, it still had little evidence for deciding which of those interactions would actually recur across different bound ligands.

So we changed what the agent could observe. Instead of asking it to infer conservation entirely from a single receptor structure, we gave it a way to run small computational experiments. The agent could design **probe molecules**, use Boltz to predict their bound poses, and inspect the ligand-side pharmacophore features produced by each probe. By comparing several probes, it could build a small computational ligand ensemble of its own and look for interaction regions that appeared repeatedly.

The harness still did not choose which probes to generate or which recurring features to trust. Its role was to expose evidence that was closer to what the task actually asks about, while leaving the scientific decisions to the model.

<IpdHarnessFigure />

The effect was much broader than the two newly solved tasks alone suggest. On the same 25 Qwen 3.5 9B tasks, point recovery increased from **10/75** to **30/75**, while full-task success went from **0/25** to **2/25**. Sixteen tasks improved, six were unchanged, and three declined.

But the intervention also changed the kind of failure we were looking at. Once the agent could recover ligand-side evidence more reliably, the remaining problem was no longer just how to infer interactions from the receptor. It was whether the model could generate informative probes, combine evidence across them, and decide how much trust to place in noisy predictions.

### What is still going wrong?

The improvement does not mean that the probing strategy itself became reliable. Once the model could generate probe molecules, the challenge shifted from extracting interactions from the receptor to **choosing useful probes and deciding what to believe from the resulting poses.**

The first problem is probe design. The model chooses its probes after inspecting the pocket, and some trajectories generated several molecules that were only minor variations of one another. In that case, apparently repeated evidence may really be several measurements of almost the same hypothesis rather than independent support for a conserved interaction.

The second problem is deciding how much to trust each probe. Boltz returns a binding probability alongside every predicted pose, but the redesigned harness currently aggregates pharmacophore features without weighting them by that probability. A feature extracted from a weakly supported pose therefore contributes just as much evidence as one from a much more credible predicted binder.

<IpdProbeConfidence />

Most of the generated probes had relatively low binding probabilities. That does not make their poses automatically useless, and IPD itself does not impose a binding-probability threshold, but it does make simple recurrence a noisy signal. **If several weak poses place the same feature in roughly the same region, repeated model error can start to look like conserved ligand evidence.**

There were also simpler search-control failures. Some trajectories stopped while Boltz calls were still available, while others spent multiple predictions around effectively the same hotspot. Both waste scarce evidence or prediction budget even after the representation problem has been improved.

At this point, the obvious next fixes start to look less like interface design and more like writing the scientific strategy ourselves. We could weight evidence by probe confidence, enforce probe diversity, encourage the model to use more of its Boltz budget, or suppress competing predictions around the same hotspot. But every one of those choices tells the agent more about **how it should run the experiment**, rather than simply giving it a better representation of the experiment.

## Lead Optimization

IPD showed how much performance can depend on the representation exposed by the harness. But not every task failed for the same reason.

Lead Optimization (LO) presents a different kind of problem. Instead of predicting a small set of 3D points, the agent runs a longer search over many related molecules, repeatedly proposing candidates, testing them, and deciding what to try next.

In an LO task, the agent starts from a protein-bound reference molecule and modifies it to improve specific properties while keeping others within required ranges. A successful candidate must also preserve binding, satisfy hard chemical constraints, and remain sufficiently similar to the starting molecule. Here, similarity is measured using Tanimoto similarity, a fingerprint-based measure of how much two molecular structures overlap.

The agent has several tools to guide this search. RDKit handles deterministic chemistry checks such as molecular validity and similarity, ADMET-AI predicts properties such as toxicity and permeability, and Boltz estimates whether a candidate is likely to maintain binding. Both ADMET and Boltz calls are limited, so the agent has to decide which candidates are worth spending those evaluations on.

### How well do current agents do?

Current models handle Lead Optimization much better than Interaction Point Discovery. On the 68-task Lite subset, GPT-5.4 and Gemini 3.1 Pro each solve **37/68** tasks, with Claude Sonnet 4.6 close behind at **36/68**. This gives us something IPD did not: a substantial mix of successful and failed trajectories on the same task family.

<LoBaselineFigure />

A terminal pass or fail, however, compresses a fairly long optimization process into a single bit. A candidate can satisfy the structural and property constraints but lose binding, preserve binding while missing one property target, or come very close to passing everything without ever becoming the submitted molecule. Looking at the evaluator stages gives us some indication of where these runs diverge. For example, GPT-5.4 and Gemini 3.1 Pro pass the Boltz binding stage on **89.7%** of tasks, while Qwen 3.5 9B does so on **54.4%**. Claude also performs strongly on the earlier checks but loses more candidates at the binding stage.

The search itself is long and state-heavy. A single trajectory can span dozens of turns while the agent proposes related analogues, evaluates different subsets of properties, discovers marginal constraint violations, tests binding, and later returns to candidates it considered much earlier. Across that process, it has to keep track of **which measurements belong to which molecule, which requirements are still untested, which candidates remain viable, and how much evaluation budget remains.**

<LoCandidateStory />

In the original harness, the model had to track all of this information entirely within its own context. There was no centralized record linking each candidate to the measurements collected for it, the constraints it passed or failed, or the evaluations that were still missing. The trajectory above shows why this becomes difficult. **A single molecular edit can repair one requirement while creating a new bottleneck, and the agent has to carry that updated state forward across many later turns.**

Looking across the failed trajectories, this became a recurring pattern. The models could often propose reasonable candidates, but struggled to reliably keep track of what had already been tested, which evidence belonged to which molecule, and which candidate was actually strongest by the end of the search. What initially looked like a molecular-design problem was therefore also a state-management problem.

### What are the agents getting wrong?

Across the failed trajectories, the same general problem appeared at several different points in the search: **the scientific state did not remain consistent over time.**

A model could test a promising molecule, correctly discover that it failed a requirement, and then submit that same molecule later. When several related candidates were explored in parallel, measurements from one could be carried over to another, or an earlier candidate could disappear from consideration entirely. Even the task specification itself could drift. A one-sided constraint might later be remembered as a two-sided interval, changing which molecules the model considered viable.

<LoFailureMap />

The same issue showed up near the end of the search. A model might make a final structural edit and submit the new molecule without rechecking every requirement, or treat a single borderline Boltz result as stronger evidence than it really was. The chemistry could remain useful while the state surrounding it became inconsistent.

These failures have different surface forms, but they share the same underlying burden. In the original harness, **candidate identity, task constraints, measurements, remaining evidence, and final verification all lived implicitly across the model’s context and tool outputs.** As the trajectory grew longer, the model had to continually reconstruct the current state of the search for itself.

That suggested a different role for the harness. Rather than telling the model which molecules to design, we could move the parts of the search that are deterministic (candidate identity, constraint bookkeeping, evidence provenance, and submission checks) into the environment, while leaving molecular design and experimental choices to the model.

### Fixing the state

The failures suggested a fairly simple division of responsibility. We still wanted the model to decide which molecules to make, which experiments were worth running, and how to respond to the results. But there was little reason for it to also reconstruct every threshold, remember which measurement belonged to which analogue, or piece together the evidence for a finalist from dozens of earlier turns.

So we moved that state out of the trajectory and into the harness.

The task requirements were compiled once into an immutable specification with explicit thresholds and optimization goals. Every unique molecule was assigned a persistent candidate ID, and its measurements were attached to that candidate in a central ledger. Deterministic checks such as molecular validity and constraint margins were computed by the harness, while ADMET and Boltz results were recorded directly against the exact molecule they evaluated.

<LoStateHarnessFigure />

Long trajectories created a second problem. Even with the scientific state stored explicitly, the conversational history could become large enough that earlier reasoning and failed approaches were difficult to recover. Inspired by Agent Rosetta’s ([Teneggi et al., 2026](#teneggi-et-al-2026)) approach to summarizing long scientific trajectories,<Sidenote number="7">Agent Rosetta keeps long protein-design trajectories manageable by maintaining a compact summary of earlier actions and their effects on key design metrics, while keeping only the two most recent interaction steps in the model’s active context. We borrow the same general idea of separating long-term trajectory information from the recent local context, but keep our structured candidate ledger outside the compressed conversation entirely.</Sidenote> once the context grew beyond roughly 60K tokens, we asked the same model to compress the older trajectory into a summary of what had been tried, what had been learned, and what remained worth exploring. The most recent actions stayed verbatim, while the candidate ledger remained untouched.

This distinction mattered. **We could compress the model’s memory of the search without compressing the scientific record itself.** Older reasoning could be summarized, but candidate identities, measurements, failed constraints, and missing evidence remained exact.

Submission became stateful as well. Instead of asking the model to write an arbitrary molecule to `solution.smi`, the redesigned harness required it to submit a registered candidate directly. A normal submission was accepted only when that exact molecule had complete passing evidence. An incomplete candidate could still be forced through as a fallback, but doing so became an explicit choice rather than an accidental mismatch between what had been tested and what was submitted.

The harness still did not choose which molecule to propose, which candidate deserved an expensive evaluation, or what chemical edit to try next. **It made the facts of the search durable while leaving the scientific decisions to the model.**

### Did better state management help?

For Qwen 3.5 9B, the change was substantial. On the same 68-task Lead Optimization Lite subset, successful tasks increased from **13/68** to **33/68**, raising the pass rate from **19.1%** to **48.5%**.

What makes the result more interesting is that V2 did not get there by submitting more often. Under the original harness, Qwen produced a terminal submission on 66 of 68 tasks, but only 13 passed every requirement. Under V2, it submitted on just 48 tasks: **33 were verified successes, 9 were explicit forced submissions with a known failed requirement, and 6 were forced with required evidence still missing.** The remaining 20 trajectories reached the turn limit without submitting.

<LoHarnessOutcomes />

So the redesigned harness made the agent much more selective. Success among submitted candidates increased from **19.7%** to **68.8%**, while overall task success improved by **29.4 percentage points**. The task-level result is the cleaner comparison, since the original harness did not distinguish forced or incomplete submissions before accepting them.

The gain also did not come from simply giving the model more room to reason. Across the 68 tasks, V2 used **12% fewer turns, 26% fewer tokens, and 17% fewer ADMET calls**, while Boltz usage increased by **51%**. With cheap checks and candidate state handled explicitly, the model spent less effort reconstructing the search and more of its expensive evaluation budget testing whether promising candidates actually maintained binding.

There was still a clear limit. **Twenty V2 trajectories reached the turn limit without submitting, compared with only two under V1.** Making the search state reliable could prevent many avoidable mistakes, but it could not tell the model how to escape an unproductive chemical search or which modification to try next.

Lead Optimization produced a different dynamic than IPD. In IPD, expanding the evidence the model could observe yielded the largest gain. Here, much of the necessary evidence already existed, and the larger gain came from keeping that data **persistent, candidate-specific, and hard to lose.** The next task explores what happens when neither intervention proves sufficient.

## Scaffold Hopping

Scaffold Hopping asks the agent to do something almost opposite to Lead Optimization. Instead of staying close to the reference molecule, the new candidate must be **structurally different enough to count as a new scaffold while preserving the way the original molecule interacts with the protein pocket.**

Those two goals pull in opposite directions. Moving farther away in chemical space makes it easier to satisfy the novelty requirement, but also makes it easier to disrupt the interactions and geometry that supported binding in the first place.

<LoVsScaffoldHopFigure />

Lead Optimization therefore rewards controlled local change. Scaffold Hopping asks the agent to **change the molecular scaffold while preserving pocket behaviour.**

### How well do current agents do?

Almost not at all. Across the 52 Scaffold Hopping tasks, no evaluated model solves more than two. Claude Sonnet 4.6, DeepSeek V3.2, GPT-5.4, and our additional Qwen 3.5 9B baseline each solve **2/52**, while the remaining models solve one task or none.

<ScaffoldHopBaselineFigure />

This is a sharp contrast with Lead Optimization, where current models solve a substantial fraction of the benchmark. Here, the best strict pass rate is only **3.8%**.

The few successes are also not concentrated on the same handful of tasks. Across all eight models, **nine different tasks are solved at least once**, with different models occasionally finding different successful scaffold hops. So the benchmark does not appear to contain one tiny universally solvable subset surrounded by impossible cases. Instead, useful solutions seem possible in several places, but current agents reach them only rarely.

That leaves an important question open: **are agents failing because they cannot generate viable scaffold changes, or because promising candidates are being lost somewhere later in the search?**

### What are the agents getting wrong?

The terminal scores make Scaffold Hopping look uniformly hopeless, but the individual evaluation checks reveal a much clearer pattern.

Generating a structurally distinct molecule is usually not the bottleneck. Models regularly clear the Tanimoto and scaffold-novelty thresholds, and several also propose candidates with high predicted binding probability.

The consistent failure is preserving the **reference interaction pattern inside the pocket.** No model clears this requirement on more than 3 of the 52 tasks. GPT-5.4 passes the binding-probability threshold on **46/52** tasks, yet preserves the required pocket interactions on only **3/52**. Claude is even more revealing: it satisfies the scaffold-novelty requirements on all 52 tasks and clears binding on 48, but preserves the reference interaction pattern on just 2.

So the problem is not simply finding novel chemistry that can still bind. Agents can often do both of those things independently. The difficult part is finding a **different scaffold that preserves the same interaction geometry in the pocket.**

### Exposing binding geometry to the agent

One problem was how the original harness presented Boltz results. The binding probability was returned directly to the model, while the structural evidence needed to inspect the predicted binding mode remained inside generated structure files. Once the agent found a sufficiently novel molecule with a high binding probability, it was therefore much easier to answer **whether it bound** than **whether it bound in the same way as the reference.**

But those are different requirements. Verifying a scaffold hop meant comparing the reference complex with the candidate’s predicted poses and reasoning about whether the important protein–ligand interactions had been preserved. Under the original interface, the model had to locate the generated structure files and reconstruct that comparison for itself.

We redesigned the harness to make this evidence easier to access. As in Lead Optimization, proposed molecules were registered under persistent candidate IDs and passed through deterministic novelty checks before spending a Boltz call. The protein and pocket configuration were also bound directly to the task.

The larger change came after Boltz. Instead of returning a scalar binding score alongside a directory of generated structures, V2 attached the result directly to the candidate and surfaced the predicted pose files explicitly.<Sidenote number="9" align="paragraph"><BoltzInterfaceNote /></Sidenote> The agent could now move directly from asking **whether a candidate appeared to bind** to inspecting **how it was predicted to bind.**

The harness still did not calculate the scaffold-hopping interaction score or tell the model which contacts had to survive. Deciding whether a pose preserved the important interactions, how to modify a candidate when it did not, and whether another experiment was worth running remained part of the model’s scientific reasoning. Final submission was also tied to the same registered candidate state rather than an independently written molecule.

This gave us a cleaner test of the remaining problem: **if the agent could directly inspect both structural novelty and predicted binding geometry, would that be enough to turn promising candidates into successful scaffold hops?**

### Did exposing the binding geometry help?

The redesigned harness made the Scaffold Hopping workflow much cleaner, but it did not improve terminal success.

Under the original harness, Qwen 3.5 9B reached submission on **39/52** tasks. V2 reached submission on **51/52** and produced valid evaluated candidates on 50. It also moved substantially more tasks through the earlier requirements: scaffold-novelty passes increased from **36** to **50**, while candidates clearing the binding-probability threshold doubled from **14** to **28**.

<ScaffoldHopHarnessOutcomes />

But those gains stopped at the interaction-preservation gate. Terminal success did not improve, moving from **2/52 under V1 to 1/52 under V2.** The same pattern appears one step earlier: three V1 candidates preserved the required reference interaction pattern, compared with only one under V2.

The two harnesses did not simply recover the same easy cases either. V2 solved a new task, but lost both tasks solved under the original harness. So the cleaner interface changed which parts of the search succeeded without producing a reliable improvement in the final scientific objective.

This is very different from Lead Optimization. There, externalizing state removed enough avoidable failure to produce a large gain. Here, exposing the predicted poses made it easier for the model to inspect what had happened, but **seeing the binding geometry was not the same as knowing how to preserve it.** The model still had to decide which interactions mattered, which structural changes would retain them, and how to redesign the scaffold when they were lost.

The redesigned harness was also more efficient. It used roughly 40% fewer tokens and 22% fewer turns, while reaching substantially more submissions and using slightly fewer Boltz calls. So the lack of improvement was not simply the result of giving the model less opportunity to search.

At this point, the obvious next interventions start to move beyond representation and execution. We could explicitly extract the reference interactions, score how closely candidate poses reproduce them, or suggest structural edits that restore missing contacts. But each of those choices encodes more of the Scaffold Hopping strategy into the harness itself.

That is the limit this task exposed. A harness can streamline search execution, clarify evidence inspection, and eliminate accidental operational failures without necessarily improving the model's scientific choices. **V2 made the remaining bottleneck far easier to see, but it did not solve it.**

## What was the human doing all this time?

Looking back, the final interventions are easy to summarize. IPD needed better ligand-side evidence. Lead Optimization needed durable candidate state. Scaffold Hopping needed easier access to predicted binding geometry.

<HarnessOptimizationLoop />

What took time was **finding the right diagnosis.**

The manual process was almost always the same: run agents, inspect failed trajectories, form a hypothesis about whether the failure came from the model or the harness, change the interface, rerun the benchmark, and inspect what broke next. Across the three task families, we went through hundreds of trajectories and repeated benchmark runs over roughly six weeks of iteration.

The difficult part was rarely implementing the eventual fix. It was deciding **what kind of failure we were looking at.**

In IPD, the model was reasoning from the wrong kind of evidence for an ensemble-derived target. In Lead Optimization, useful chemistry was often already present, but the search state degraded across a long trajectory. In Scaffold Hopping, the relevant structural information existed, but the interface made the scalar binding score much easier to use than the predicted poses needed to inspect the interaction pattern.

Those are very different diagnoses. None follows from a generic rule like “add more tools” or “write a better prompt.” The human contribution was deciding **what the harness should represent explicitly, what it should make persistent, and what should remain part of the model’s scientific policy.**

That boundary mattered because more harness engineering was not always better. Lead Optimization improved dramatically. IPD improved, then exposed a deeper probe-selection problem. Scaffold Hopping became cleaner and more efficient without improving terminal success. A harness change could remove accidental difficulty, or it could start encoding the scientific strategy itself.

Manual harness engineering was therefore far more than mere implementation work. It was an active search over **representations, interfaces, and the boundaries between model and environment.**

**If harness engineering is itself a search process, how much of that search can we automate?**

## Can the harness engineer itself?

There are already several ways to automate parts of harness engineering, including prompt optimizers such as GEPA. But our manual interventions touched much more than the prompt: tools, state representation, compaction, oracle wrappers, and submission logic all changed across the three tasks. We therefore wanted a method where the **entire executable harness** could be part of the search space.

For this, we used **Meta-Harness**, which treats harness design itself as an iterative optimization problem.

<MetaHarnessOverviewFigure />

The loop is straightforward. A meta-agent sees the source code, execution traces, and evaluation scores from previous harnesses, proposes a new version, evaluates it on the task, stores the resulting traces and score, and repeats. Because the harness itself is executable code, the optimizer is free to change much more than instructions alone.

We initialized this search from the same baseline harness used in our manual experiments and asked whether automated search could recover the kinds of improvements we had spent weeks finding by hand.

There is one boundary we kept fixed. The optimizer could redesign how public task information was represented and how the agent interacted with its environment, but it could not expose hidden evaluator answers or directly encode task-specific solutions. The scientific policy still had to belong to the child agent.

There was also a more personal reason I found this setup interesting. Throughout the manual phase, Claude Opus 5 had already been my main workhorse for reading trajectories, organizing failure modes, and brainstorming possible harness changes. I was still choosing which hypotheses to pursue, deciding where the model–harness boundary should sit, and implementing the final interventions, but a capable model was already involved in much of the analysis.

Meta-Harness therefore made the comparison more direct. If essentially the same kind of model could inspect the traces, reason about what was going wrong, edit the harness itself, and evaluate the result, **how much was I actually contributing to the loop?** Was the human mainly providing taste and judgment that the model could not recover on its own, or was I just manually orchestrating a process that could already be automated?

### How did we run the search?

To make the comparison fair, we kept the downstream scientific agent fixed. Qwen 3.5 9B served as the model solving the SMDD tasks, while Claude Opus acted as the meta-agent optimizing the harness. This setup created a direct test against the human baseline: Opus had already been my primary tool for analyzing trajectories and brainstorming manual fixes. The question was whether the meta-agent still needed a human in the loop to translate diagnoses into working code.

For each task family, the automated search started from the same original harness rather than from our manually improved version. The meta-agent could inspect the harness source code, trajectories produced by previous candidates, and their evaluation results, then propose a new executable harness for the next round.

We placed very few restrictions on how it could improve that harness. Prompts were editable, but so were the tools themselves, how state was represented, what information tool calls returned, how context was managed, and the control logic around submission. In other words, the search space included essentially everything around the child model that we had spent the previous sections modifying by hand.

What remained untouched was equally important. The child model was fixed, the underlying SMDD tasks and scientific oracles stayed constant, and the optimizer received no hidden answers or task-specific shortcuts. A candidate harness earned reward only by helping Qwen solve the task directly.

We ran this search independently for each of the three task families. Each search used 20 optimization tasks, with four rollouts per task, giving 80 trajectories for every candidate harness. Starting from the base harness, Meta-Harness then proposed 10 additional iterations, so each task family required 11 harness evaluations × 80 trajectories = 880 trajectories. Across all three task families, the automated search therefore generated 2,640 child-agent trajectories.

We deliberately kept the optimization signal simple: every Meta-Harness run was scored only by **terminal task success**. We did not construct task-specific reward rubrics or shape the objective using the failure modes we had discovered manually. That made the comparison cleaner—the automated optimizer received the same definition of success across tasks, rather than an additional layer of human engineering in the reward itself.

This was a deliberately conservative choice for studying harness search, not necessarily the reward we would choose if the only goal were maximum benchmark performance.

## Human taste vs automated search

The automated optimizer started with the same raw ingredients as the human process (code, trajectories, error traces, and iterative attempts) while receiving none of our explicit diagnoses about what to fix.

So, **which harness worked better?**

**Manual engineering came out ahead overall, but not on every task.**

<HumanVsAHEFigure />

The largest advantage was in Lead Optimization. Manual V2 solved 33 of 68 tasks, compared with 13 under both the original harness and automated search. On the paired comparison, V2 uniquely solved 26 tasks while AHE uniquely solved six. The automated optimizer had therefore failed to produce a meaningful improvement over the starting harness, while the manual redesign recovered a large fraction of the benchmark.

Interaction Point Discovery showed the same direction, although the signal was better expressed through partial credit. The original harness recovered 10 of 75 hidden points, automated search recovered 12, and Manual V2 recovered 30. V2 improved the point count on 16 of the 25 paired tasks and regressed on only three. Complete solutions remained rare (two under V2 and none under AHE), so the stronger result here is not the terminal solve count, but the much broader recovery of correct interaction points.

Scaffold Hopping was the exception. Automated harness search produced the strongest observed Scaffold Hopping result, reaching 5/52 compared with 2/52 for V1 and 1/52 for Manual V2. The absolute numbers are small, and the paired terminal difference is not statistically decisive. The direction remains revealing nonetheless, as this was the only task family where automated search found a harness that improved over both the starting point and our manual redesign.

The more useful distinction was what kind of change helped. Our largest gains came from fixing representation and state, while Scaffold Hopping improved when automated search changed the search strategy itself.

### What did the automated optimizer actually learn?

Across all three tasks, Claude did not converge on one generic recipe for making Qwen more reliable. Each run developed its own sequence of hypotheses, reacting to failures in the previous harness and gradually changing both the workflow and the instructions given to the child model.

Lead Optimization was the clearest case where automated search converged toward many of the same problems we had identified manually. The base harness left almost all of the bookkeeping inside the trajectory. Qwen had to remember the task requirements, keep track of which measurements belonged to which molecule, decide what was still missing, and make sure the final submission corresponded to the candidate it had actually evaluated.

Meta-Harness started attacking this quickly. By iteration 1, it had introduced a staged cheap-to-expensive workflow, a running candidate table, persistent memory for candidates and hypotheses, budget checkpoints, and explicit instructions to measure candidates before moving on. This made trajectories considerably more structured and shorter, although it did not yet improve correctness.

The next iteration made the procedure progressively harder to violate. Iteration 2 stopped Qwen from freely typing candidate SMILES, required candidates to be generated through RDKit transformations, required objective measurements before submission, and added an explicit candidate-level gate table. It also verified that the exact molecule written to the output file was the one that had been measured. This became the highest-scoring harness observed during the automated search and was the version we retained for the final benchmark comparison.

Claude later went even further. By iteration 4, every requirement was explicitly routed to its intended measurement source, unknown gates had to be measured before submission, known failures were supposed to keep the search running, and fully passing candidates were ranked by their weakest margin. In other words, the optimizer had independently arrived at much of the same high-level diagnosis: Lead Optimization was failing because evidence from different candidates and different gates was not being integrated reliably at the final decision.

<AheVsHumanStateFigure />

The search dynamics were stepwise and noisy rather than monotonic. Most of the gain arrived with the iteration-2 finalist and submission checks. Later harnesses sometimes solved a broader set of tasks, but none surpassed that iteration's trajectory-level reward.

Meta-Harness gave Qwen a progressively better procedure for maintaining state. It also introduced persistent memory and control machinery, so this was more than simply rewriting the prompt. But candidate identity, requirement completion, and final selection still ultimately depended on Qwen applying that machinery correctly.

Our Manual V2 redesign moved those responsibilities one level further out. The task specification was compiled once and made immutable. Every molecule received a canonical candidate ID. ADMET and Boltz results were attached to that ID in an append-only ledger rather than reconstructed from conversation history. Gate status and signed margins were computed from that stored evidence, and a normal submission was rejected unless every required gate was known and passing. Compaction could summarize the trajectory, but it could not rewrite the candidate state underneath it.

That distinction is subtle, but important. Both approaches recognized the bookkeeping problem. Automated search made the bookkeeping easier for Qwen to perform, while Manual V2 made less of the bookkeeping Qwen's responsibility in the first place.

The automated search was also noisy. The chosen iteration achieved 37.5% in its initial 80-rollout evaluation and 27.5% upon re-evaluation, while another duplicate run shifted from 33.75% to 28.75%. The optimization trajectory was therefore noisy rather than monotonic, with substantial variance even between identical harnesses. Some of that instability is likely exacerbated by the sparsity of the terminal reward, especially on tasks where complete success is rare.

IPD diverged more sharply. Unlike Lead Optimization, the automated optimizer did not converge on the same abstraction we had chosen manually. Instead, it kept trying to make Qwen better at inferring the hidden pharmacophore directly from receptor geometry.

Early changes focused on execution hygiene and task management. Iteration 2 stripped down an overcomplicated tool flow, guided Qwen to log a tentative answer early, and tightened turn-budget tracking. Iteration 3 replaced isolated hotspot rankings with whole-triplet scoring, enforcing limits on centroid location, inter-point distances, and spatial spread. By iteration 7, Claude introduced burial-weighted PCA to identify the main cavity axis and disperse the three points across a ligand-sized envelope. This setup achieved the best partial recovery in the automated run, identifying 43 of 240 points across 80 rollouts, while zero-shot complete solutions stayed rare.

There is an important asymmetry in what the optimizer could learn from these runs. Meta-Harness only received binary terminal success as its reward. In IPD, a trajectory recovering two of the three hidden points received the same reward as one recovering none: zero. The partial-point metrics we use here to understand the search trajectory were diagnostic measurements computed afterward, not optimization signal available to Claude.

<IpdAheVsHumanFigure />

That choice was intentional. Giving IPD a handcrafted partial-credit reward, Lead Optimization a gate-margin rubric, and Scaffold Hopping a weighted combination of novelty, binding, and interaction preservation would introduce another substantial piece of human design into the automated side of the comparison. But if the objective were simply to produce the strongest possible harness, richer rewards are an obvious direction. IPD in particular could expose matched-point recovery directly, while other tasks could use evaluator-derived rubrics or graded gate progress to provide much denser feedback.

So the automated search was clearly adapting to the task. It recognized that individually plausible interaction sites were not enough, that the three points had to form a coherent molecular-scale configuration, and that spatial coverage of the pocket mattered. But all of these interventions were still variations on the same basic strategy: infer the missing ligand geometry from the receptor.

Our manual redesign tackled the core representation directly. First, we made the output semantics explicit. Predictions represented ligand-side features, receptor donors mapped to ligand acceptors, repeated feature types were permitted, matching was strictly one-to-one, and predictions had to land within 2.5 Å of a target point. We also replaced manual file formatting with a structured pharmacophore submission interface.

The larger change was giving the model a way to generate its own ligand-side evidence. Qwen could propose probe molecules, predict their binding poses with Boltz, and extract aligned pharmacophore features such as Donors, Acceptors, Aromatics, Hydrophobics, Cations, and Anions. Across the final evaluation runs, this generated 138 successful unique probes and 694 ligand-feature records, with 23 of 25 tasks gathering evidence from multiple molecules. In contrast, the automated search generated zero valid molecular Boltz probes across its 880 optimization rollouts, even after probe generation appeared in later search iterations.

That distinction showed up clearly in the final paired evaluation. The selected automated harness recovered 12 of 75 hidden points, while Human V2 recovered 30. Tasks with at least one correct point rose from 9 to 20 out of 25, and tasks with at least two correct points grew from 3 to 8. Complete solutions remained rare (zero under AHE versus two under V2), but the shift in partial-credit recovery was unmistakable. Both configurations submitted on all 25 tasks, with V2 requiring fewer turns on average despite executing 146 Boltz predictions.

Automated search became increasingly sophisticated at reasoning from the receptor. Human V2 changed what the model was allowed to observe. Instead of asking Qwen to infer conserved ligand interactions from one protein structure, the harness gave it a crude ligand ensemble from which those interactions could be inferred.

<ScaffoldAheVsHumanFigure />

Scaffold Hopping reversed the pattern. Here, automated search produced the stronger harness, but not because it made the workflow cleaner in the same way as our manual redesign. The two approaches ended up putting pressure on different parts of the task.

The base harness made binding probability easy to observe, but preserving the reference binding mode was much harder. Candidate poses existed, yet the model still had to inspect them and determine whether key interactions remained intact. This created an obvious failure mode. The model would find a novel structure, observe a strong binding score, and terminate the search early despite disrupting the original contact network.

Meta-Harness gradually pushed against that behavior. Early iterations introduced task-bound candidate tools, cheap screening, and candidate memory. Later policies became more conservative about the chemical change itself, explicitly preserving anchors and linker geometry while changing the core, and requiring candidates to be measured before submission. The selected harness then reintroduced pose analysis after prediction and warned against drifting back toward the reference while optimizing binding. The result was not a monotonic improvement, but the best policies were clearly trying to balance novelty against interaction preservation rather than optimize either one in isolation.

Our manual V2 redesign went in a different direction. We made the deterministic parts of the task much harder to get wrong. Every candidate received a canonical identity, novelty was checked with evaluator-matched Tanimoto and scaffold-MCS calculations, malformed or non-novel candidates could be rejected before an expensive Boltz call, and predicted pose files were returned explicitly and attached to the tested candidate. The model no longer had to hunt through filesystem outputs just to inspect its own prediction.

The important limitation was what the harness could actually enforce. Normal submission required the exposed novelty and binding gates to pass, but the evaluator's interaction-similarity score remained hidden. Pose inspection and model-written interaction evidence were available, but they were advisory rather than a blocking gate. A force=false submission could therefore be completely valid according to the public harness state and still fail the benchmark because the binding interaction pattern had changed.

That is exactly what happened in the paired evaluation. Human V2 passed both novelty gates on all 50 evaluable submissions, compared with 39/45 for Tanimoto and 32/45 for scaffold MCS under AHE. But the interaction gate went the other way: 8/45 AHE submissions preserved the required interaction pattern, compared with only 1/50 under V2. Terminal success followed that difference, with AHE solving 5 of 52 tasks and Human V2 solving 1 of 52.

The result is therefore a useful limit case for manual harness engineering. We made novelty exact, candidate identity reliable, Boltz execution task-bound, and pose evidence easier to inspect, but none of those changes could make the hidden interaction requirement enforceable without moving further into the scientific policy itself. Automated search was willing to do exactly that: it increasingly shaped how Qwen should modify the molecule and what trade-offs it should preserve.

Across the three tasks, automated search was clearly capable of finding useful harness changes, but it did not simply rediscover the manual designs. In Lead Optimization it converged on similar bookkeeping failures but left more of the procedure in Qwen’s hands. In IPD it kept refining how Qwen reasoned from receptor geometry, while the manual harness changed the evidence available altogether. And in Scaffold Hopping, the direction flipped: the automated optimizer was willing to encode a stronger chemical search policy, and that produced the better harness. The remaining question, then, is less whether harness engineering can be automated and more where we want the harness to stop and the model’s scientific policy to begin.

## Conclusion

So, is human taste overrated in harness engineering?

Not entirely. But its value depends heavily on what part of the problem we are trying to optimize.

When the failure came from the way the task was represented to the model, what evidence it could access, or what state it was expected to maintain, the strongest gains came from human-designed abstractions. Lead Optimization improved when candidate identity and evidence stopped living inside the trajectory. Interaction Point Discovery improved when the harness stopped asking the model to infer conserved ligand behavior from a single receptor and instead exposed ligand-side evidence directly.

Automated search was much more competitive once the problem shifted from making the task legible to finding a better policy within an already usable interface. Scaffold Hopping was the clearest example. Our manual harness made novelty exact and pose evidence easier to inspect, but Meta-Harness was more willing to shape how Qwen searched the chemical space itself, and ultimately produced the stronger harness.

That suggests a useful division of labor. Human judgment still matters most when deciding what should be represented explicitly, what should be enforced by the environment, and what should remain the model’s responsibility. Once those choices are sensible, automated optimization can search over workflows, instructions, heuristics, and control policies far more cheaply than repeatedly doing the same loop by hand.

A natural stopping point exists. Interventions that maintain deterministic state or surface accessible task data belong to infrastructure. When the harness prescribes chemical transformations, prioritizes specific contacts, or negotiates scientific trade-offs, it crosses into policy design. Further progress is then better achieved by fine-tuning or training the model directly rather than hardcoding scientific reasoning into the harness.

So human taste is not disappearing from harness engineering. It is moving up a level. The interesting question is becoming less “What prompt or tool should I add?” and more “Where should the boundary between the environment and the scientist actually be?” Once that boundary is chosen well, much of the optimization inside it may not need a human at all.

## Final thoughts

SMDD-Bench has been useful precisely because many of its failures are difficult to classify cleanly as either “the model is bad at chemistry” or “the agent system is badly designed.” A model can propose chemically sensible candidates and still fail because it loses track of evidence; conversely, a perfectly organized harness can expose the remaining scientific bottleneck without actually solving it. That ambiguity makes the benchmark frustrating to work with, but also unusually useful for studying where scientific-agent capability actually lives.

There is still a lot we have not resolved. Interaction Point Discovery remains extremely sparse at full-task success, Scaffold Hopping still struggles to preserve binding interactions reliably, and several of the manual interventions described here have not yet been explored across every model or task family. We also only studied a subset of SMDD-Bench in depth. Other task families, including 2D Pharmacophore Identification and Fragment Assembly, may expose very different failure modes and may favor different kinds of harness intervention altogether. More broadly, the human-versus-automated patterns we observed here should be tested across a wider set of models, harnesses, task instances, and optimization procedures before treating them as general properties of scientific agents. Our experiments therefore probe only one part of a much larger space between changing the environment and changing the model. Once the harness has made the task legible and execution reliable, the obvious next step is to ask whether training the policy itself produces larger and more transferable gains than continuing to encode scientific strategy around it.

The optimization objective is another axis we have barely explored. We intentionally used binary terminal success throughout these experiments to avoid embedding our own task-specific diagnoses into the automated reward. That is useful for the comparison here, but probably leaves performance on the table. Future runs could test denser evaluator-derived signals, rubric-based rewards, or task-specific partial credit—such as the number of correctly recovered interaction points in IPD—and ask whether automated harness search becomes more competitive once it receives a better learning signal.
